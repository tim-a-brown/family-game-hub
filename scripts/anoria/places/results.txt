ANORIA: PLACES AS EACH PLAYER'S OWN STACK. Test games, 2026-11-11.
scripts/anoria/places/run-places.js, on engine-places.js (a copy of the game's engine: with no stacks it plays the
same games as games/anoria/engine.js, checked game for game).

HOW THE GAMES WERE PLAYED
  Every game is first to 25 Renown or three Seals, with the game's other rules as they stand (STD in engine.js).
  A cell is the five ready decks (40 cards) against each other, all 20 pairings, both seats, each deck played in its
  own style, as scripts/anoria/variant.js does. 2,000 games a cell unless a table says otherwise.
  Each deck has a themed stack of 12 Places from places.json (60 Places, seven domains).
  Noise: on 2,000 games a share is good to about 1.1 points either way, and on 1,600 to about 1.3. One deck's win rate
  (800 of the 2,000 games) is good to about 1.8. The spread between the five decks moves by chance alone: today's game
  measures 4.1 on one set of 2,000 games and 8.5 on another. Read a spread under 8 as "about the same as today".
  The robots choose by Might, Renown, Seals and a rough value for each Place rule. They are steady, not clever: a
  number here is a comparison between rules, not a forecast of what people will do.

THE RULES THESE GAMES RECOMMEND (RULES.md is the sheet for the table)
  - Each player brings a stack of 12 Places, no two the same. The same Place may be in both players' stacks, but it
    lies on the table only once: the second one turned up goes under its stack.
  - Set-up: draw three, lay one at your Gate and one at your Outpost, offer the third for the Crossroads.
  - First dibs: each player rolls a die. The higher roll's offer is the Crossroads. The other player's offer starts
    their Place discard pile, and that player takes the first turn.
  - The Crossroads card lies on its owner's side of the seam, facing its owner. If the Might there is tied and its
    owner has a Figure there, its owner holds it. Its rule works for both players.
  - A replaced Place goes face up on its owner's Place discard pile. Figures, a Royal Decree and Imhotep's count stay.
    The new rule works at once. An empty stack is made again from the discard pile.
  - A Commerce Place pays at an Outpost or the Crossroads, not at a Gate. A Law Place keeps your last Seal.
  - Domination: with four or more domains on the table, holding a Place of every one at the end of a round wins.

  What it says.
  - With no edge the owner wins 51.5%: supplying the Crossroads from a stack that suits your deck is worth a point
    or two by itself, no more.
  - +1 Might there is far too much: 65%. One die roll before the game would decide it.
  - A rule that works only for its owner is a real edge, 55 to 56%. It is still a die roll deciding five points, and
    it makes several rules awkward to read (a road only one player may use).
  - Winning ties while you have a Figure there is 52%, within the noise of no edge. It never decides a game by
    itself, it is one short sentence, and it is the same rule Cleopatra's deck already teaches.
  - Winning ties with nobody there is worse than no edge for its owner (46.8%). It hands them the Crossroads from
    round 1, they lead early, and the game's own catch-up rules (the leader goes first, the player behind gets a
    Spark, the bounty) work against them all game.
  - Nothing tried "in return" moves the result by more than the noise. The first turn is the tidy one: one roll then
    settles both questions a table has at the start, whose Place is the Crossroads and who goes first.
  Recommended: the owner wins ties at the Crossroads while they have a Figure there, and the other player takes the
  first turn. First dibs then wins 50.8%.

  Two rules were changed on the strength of the first run of this table.
  - Commerce at a Gate. An empty Gate is held by its owner, so "whoever holds it gets 1 more Spark" at your own Gate
    was a Spark every turn for nothing: its owner won 58.9% (575 games). "Figures arriving here cost 1 less" at your
    Gate, where most Figures arrive, won 59.9% (289). Today's game has both (Stonehenge and Timbuktu are dealt to
    Gates). Paying only a player with a Figure standing there, and only the first Figure a turn, barely helped (57.0%
    and 61.9%): a player just leaves a cheap Figure at home. Commerce paying nothing at a Gate brings Spark to 52.2%;
    the cheaper Figures are still at 57%, which with 289 games is not yet proof of anything, and wants a second look.
  - Law. "While you hold it, your Seals cannot be broken" saved 0.40 Seals a game, cut Seal wins to 24.6% and cost
    Pathfinders, the raiding deck, 1 to 2 points. "Your last Seal cannot be broken" saves 0.10 a game and leaves Seal
    wins at 27 to 29% (1,600 games each). The last Seal is the one that ends the game, so the rule is still felt.
  Still out of line: a Law Place at your own Gate does nothing (41%: it belongs at the Outpost), Land's fort is on the
  weak side everywhere, and Arts Figures' +1 Might is on the strong side everywhere.

  What it says.
  - A Renown bonus for three domains is a win-more rule. Your own Gate and Outpost are two domains already, so the
    bonus goes to whoever holds the Crossroads as well: four fifths of it to the player already ahead. Games get half
    a round shorter and the winning margin a point wider. Not recommended.
  - The same bonus at four domains hardly ever pays (once in three games) and nearly always to the leader. Nothing.
  - Doubling a domain's rules when you hold two Places of it changes nothing measurable, and it can't be said simply:
    a rule that gives Might decides who holds the Place, which decides whether the rule is doubled. Not recommended.
  - Winning by holding every domain on the table is too sudden when any table counts: 40% of games, some of them over
    in round 3 because only three domains were dealt.
  - With four or more domains on the table it is a third way to win in about one game in seven. It asks for four
    Places, one of them on the other player's side. It takes its wins from games the leader was already winning
    (98%), so it changes who wins very little and ends won games a round sooner. It is the one rule of the six that
    makes the domains matter on the table without rewarding the leader with still more Renown. Recommended, as
    "Domination", with that caution: it is a finish, not a comeback.
  The domains matter most through their own rules, which is where the test games put the weight: each domain pays in
  a different coin (Spark, cheaper cards, cards, safe ground, Renown, a safe Seal, movement), so holding three
  different ones compounds in a way holding three of one does not.

WHAT IS NOT SETTLED
  - The 60 Places have not had a balance pass of their own. Table 5 names the outliers. The ready decks' stacks will
    want the same tuning the second printing got, a few hundred games a change, before any of them is called fair.
  - The robots choose their Gate, Outpost and offer from a rough table. People will find better set-ups, and better
    uses for Earthquake and The Cape Route, than the robots do. Costs here are a floor, not a ceiling.
  - Domination was measured on one set of 1,600 games.
  - A stack that mixes freely (any 12 Places with any deck) was only played as "dealt from all 60": table 5. What a
    player who builds a stack on purpose can do with it (twelve Places of one region for a deck of that region) is
    not measured, and is the first thing to try before stacks can be built by hand.
  - Three or more players: not played. The engine's map handles them; the stacks, first dibs and the edge are written
    for two.
